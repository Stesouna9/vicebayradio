The station's story

The fortieth floor in demonstration mode

SKYLINE · 324 FRONT STREET

Fortieth floor of an office tower, formerly an oversized lift-control room; cyan NEON sign behind smoked glass, overlooking the crane and neighbouring terraces. Technical roof with worn but dry covering; vertical aerial on a reinforced base, inspection indicator intact.

Number 324 Front Street was built with a much oversized control room on the fortieth floor. Lifts occupied the shafts, technicians occupied two chairs, and the remaining space held plans nobody could unfold completely. When control operations moved to a more convenient room below, smoked glass, rows of cable channels and a light board announcing floors without transporting anyone remained. The window overlooked the Skyline crane and neighbouring terraces. The lease described the space as “immediately available”, unlike the lift on certain days. Two abandoned chairs were included, although the inventory carefully declined to assess their comfort.

In October 1985, NEON turned a loudspeaker test there into a permanent broadcast. A cassette from technical trials at Pixel Palace in Downtown supplied the opening beeps and frequency announcement. Cable channels welcomed the desk wiring; old plans became drawer liners, retained for their clean lines. The room’s insulation suited short sounds, square bass and measured silences. The floor board stayed as scenery, with a label explaining that it provided no useful information. Visitors continued checking it before asking whether the studio had arrived. An arrow was eventually added to the actual door, reducing enquiries without improving the board.

Height brought small, stubborn problems: a relay clicked behind the partition, the aerial picked up a fan’s vibrations, and calculation rolls unwound towards the door. New fastenings, relay insulation and a deep paper tray followed. In April 1986, measuring the interchange 9 queue in arcade games required a second roll. Dolores Van Pelt inaugurated its holder instead of the crane still stuck outside the window. The official photograph mostly shows the tray. The station filed this among its rare events lasting longer than the silence preceding them; the corresponding paper record extends across the back of the photograph.

Today, illuminated controls cast squares on the glass and the ventilation smells of heated dust. The terraces appear close, but their conversations never reach this height. Only sound chips, sliding paper and the technical door’s latch can be heard. The setting sun passes through Skyline, turns the crane into a black line and places a copper band across the console. The board still displays an imaginary floor. Nobody asks it to lead anywhere here: a steady frequency is already considered a destination precise enough to justify the journey upstairs.

    LITTLE STORIES

  • The floor board says “display without movement”.
  • The calculation tray holds exactly two rolls.
  • The glass retains a sound-level graduation.
  • One chair is marked “operator absent”.
  • The functionless button is the cleanest.

    THE NEIGHBOURS

  • Seabreeze Terrace : Seabreeze Terrace at 320 lends clips to hold papers near the ventilation.
  • Laser Lounge : Laser Lounge at 328 trades bulbs for old lighting diagrams from the room.
  • Future Phone : Future Phone at 248 in Downtown supplies cables during trips between the two Front Street districts.

How to find it. At 324 Front Street, take the lobby lift to the fortieth floor. Follow the exposed cable channels towards cyan-lit smoked glass, ignoring the illuminated floor board.

Who is Melvin ?

NEON 103.5: a cold voice, precise calculations, usefulness still under measurement.

Melvin’s on-air character appears in the Pixel Palace archives in Downtown on a maintenance sheet dated 1984. The “origin” field reads “audio input”. All other data remains outside the programme.

In October 1985, a speaker-test cassette announces NEON 103.5 between two sequences of beeps. The voice gives no name. The tape ends; the frequency continues, with a punctuality that embarrasses the clock.

The voice sorts silences by duration and announces a 97.314% probability that a button is round. In April 1986, it measures the interchange 9 traffic jam in Pixel Palace games. The result exceeds the paper length; a second roll receives official certification.

    GOOD TO KNOW

  • On-air equipment: a calculation roll with the total on the back.
  • The voice announces the frequency, never its name.
  • Statistics have three decimal places, including those concerning silence.

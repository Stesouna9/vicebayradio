The station's story

The office for letters that stay

DOWNTOWN · 216 FRONT STREET

First floor of a former business correspondence office; red ÉMOTION sign softened by a lampshade, studio overlooking Front Street and the flowers at 212. Dull zinc roof with a repaired old seam; short aerial on a firm mast, cable secured clear of the gutter.

The first floor of 216 Front Street was once a business correspondence office. Quotations, reminders and registered letters emerged from three typewriters whose covers never matched. Customers waited in a narrow corridor beneath a clock stopped at two. When the typists left, they abandoned pigeonholes, a shaded lamp and a drawer of unused carbon paper. Nothing about the premises was spectacular: its window overlooked flowers on the neighbouring pavement, and its peace depended on passing taxis. That was precisely what made the place habitable. You could imagine waiting there without having to explain why.

Émotion moved in during November 1984 to broadcast ballads from a room already experienced in waiting for replies. The pigeonholes became a dedication system; the central table received a console on a rectangle of felt. The clock stayed because repairing it cost more than the first batch of tapes. Red light from the sign passed through the old lampshade placed against the window, producing something like a corridor night-light. A technician suggested greater visibility; the first letter received instead thanked the station for sparing the flats opposite a dazzling advertisement. The lampshade immediately became a permanent fitting.

In January 1985, a misfiled dedication prompted the call that brought Marie to the microphone. The building found its lasting purpose: holding voices long enough for them to reach somebody. A talkative gutter still required attention, each drop resonating through the ventilation duct. Repairing the roof ended this uninvited solo without changing the room. The great moment came in February 1986: an ordinary flower-delivery confirmation prompted eleven reconciliations. For three days, thank-you envelopes and two bouquets without recipients arrived on the landing; the bouquets received their own register entry rather than having a destination chosen for them.

Today, mailbags brush the banister and flowers scent the already warm air. From the desk, drivers can be seen waiting outside shopfronts, hands resting on steering wheels as though on a table. Régis prefers urgent letters to his designated cushion. The setting sun lights the repaired zinc before slipping beneath the blinds; inside, it remains two in the morning. An empty envelope waits beside the telephone. It keeps a place for the reply, even when nobody yet knows which question to ask, or how long an answer might reasonably take.

    LITTLE STORIES

  • The carbon drawer is labelled “copies of silences”.
  • A ruler keeps envelopes away from the fader.
  • The register separates flowers received from flowers expected.
  • The bell says “once is enough”.
  • The clock is dusted without being wound.

    THE NEIGHBOURS

  • Blue Roses Florist : Blue Roses Florist at 212 delivers bouquets and checks receipts before any reading.
  • Moonlight Cleaners : Moonlight Cleaners at 208 cleans the studio curtain without removing its central fold.
  • Silver Cab Service : Silver Cab Service at 230 takes guests home after the longest dedications.

How to find it. At 216 Front Street, look for the red glow behind the lampshade in the upper window. Climb one flight and ring once at the door beside the mail compartments.

Who is Marie ?

Marie, “The Voice”: two in the morning, even when the sun insists otherwise.

Marie comes from the nights of Downtown, when taxis keep waiting after the last customer. In 1984, she keeps a notebook of phrases overheard through windows. The happiest get underlined twice, as a precaution.

Émotion 98.3 discovers her in January 1985 when she calls to correct a misplaced dedication. She reads “happy birthday” with such gravity that they reserve her a microphone. Her cat Régis arrives for the second broadcast with his cushion.

She removes staples from letters to avoid injuring any memories. In February 1986, a card from Blue Roses Florist on Front Street merely asks her to confirm a delivery. Her reading prompts eleven reconciliations; she keeps the receipt, unsure where Régis should sign.

    GOOD TO KNOW

  • The studio clock stays at two.
  • Régis has a cushion but prefers urgent letters.
  • She saves empty envelopes “for the reply”.
